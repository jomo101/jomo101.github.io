Research

Lattice quantum field theory, the statistics needed to get physics out of it, and the software that runs it.

Hadron resonances from coupled-channel scattering

A lattice calculation does not produce a resonance. It produces a discrete tower of finite-volume energies, and the physics lives in how that tower shifts with the box. The Lüscher formalism turns those shifts into scattering amplitudes, whose analytic continuation reveals poles.

With the Baryon Scattering (BaSc) collaboration I worked on the $\Lambda(1405)$, long suspected of being two poles rather than one. It needs a large variational basis — single-baryon interpolators alongside two-particle $\pi\Sigma$ and $\bar{K}N$ operators — because the states that matter are scattering states a three-quark operator barely overlaps with. Mapping that spectrum through the coupled-channel quantization condition gave the first ab initio evidence for the two-pole structure (PRL 132, 051901; PRD 109, 014511, both Editors' Suggestions). The poles are not something you measure so much as something you can only see once the basis is rich enough to hold the states.

Structure of bound states in finite volume

Given a bound state on the lattice, what can you say about how it is put together? Finite-volume matrix elements carry that information, but only after the volume dependence is separated from the physical form factor. In arXiv:2602.20373 we develop and test formalism for extracting charge radii and similar observables from lattice EFT calculations of two-body bound states, using systems where the answer is known independently as a control. I derived the estimator, validated it against analytic limits, and quantified the systematic error budget.

Two-nucleon systems at heavy quark mass

Whether the deuteron and dineutron bind at unphysically heavy pion masses has been contested for a decade. Using a large variational basis of local and bilocal interpolators, we found no bound di-nucleons at $m_\pi \approx 714$ MeV (PRC 113, 024002). The disagreement in the literature turns out to be largely a story about excited-state contamination and how honestly it is treated.

Lattice field theory software and HPC

Alongside the physics I write the simulation machinery — chiefly a Wilson-clover RHMC gauge-field sampler built from scratch in C, with every stage pinned by an identity the physics must satisfy rather than by a unit test alone.

Two results are worth stating as results. Replacing leapfrog with Omelyan 2MN and a two-level Sexton–Weingarten splitting cut the energy violation 30-fold at equal solve budget, and with fewer solves — the useful part being the diagnosis behind it, that the gauge force was setting the step-size limit. And a measured study of Clark–Kennedy $n$-th-root splitting found it does not pay at small volume and heavy quarks: $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} N_{\mathrm{mv}}$ rises monotonically with $n$ because the integrator error is gauge-dominated. A negative result, but a quantitative one, and it redirected the effort.

Bayesian inference for thermonuclear reaction rates

Before the lattice I worked on nuclear astrophysics: hierarchical Bayesian fits to $D(p,\gamma)^3$He cross-section data, propagating experimental systematics into the reaction rate that sets the primordial deuterium abundance (ApJ 923, 49). The datasets spanned decades and were mutually inconsistent; rather than discarding outliers by hand, the model infers a systematic offset per dataset. I still use the machinery — most of what I do now is the same problem in different notation.

Current directions

I am working my way into quantum simulation of gauge theories. Real-time dynamics and finite density are where the Monte Carlo approach runs out of road. This is a direction rather than a track record; nothing published yet.
